Applications · Motivation letter / SOP

Write the document the programme asks for—not a generic “winning SOP.”

European universities and scholarship programmes use different names, lengths and evaluation criteria for motivation documents. Start with the official requirement, then build a concise evidence-based argument for fit.

Reviewed: 11 September 2026Reference check: DAAD and uni-assist6 min read

There is no universal European SOP format

Some programmes ask for a statement of purpose, some for a motivation letter, some for a study plan, and some ask specific questions instead. Lengths and required content vary. DAAD scholarship calls, for example, can specify their own page limits and content expectations, while uni-assist notes that a motivation letter explains why you are applying to a particular university and programme and why you are suited to it.

The programme requirement outranks every template. If a university asks for 1,000 words answering three prompts, a beautiful two-page generic SOP is still the wrong document.

The evidence-first structure

1. Direction

Open by stating the academic or technical direction you are pursuing and the problem space that motivates it. Avoid childhood-origin stories unless they directly explain a meaningful decision or project.

2. Evidence

Use selected academic, project, research or professional evidence to show what you have already done. Instead of listing every subject or tool, explain one or two technically relevant experiences and what you learned from them.

3. Programme fit

Connect your next learning need to specific elements of the programme: modules, research groups, facilities, industrial links or methodological strengths. Use only elements you have actually verified on the institution’s current site.

4. Future use

Explain how the programme advances a credible next step. This does not need to be a rigid five-year prediction; it should show that the degree fits into a coherent technical or professional direction.

What DAAD guidance reinforces

DAAD’s scholarship guidance asks applicants to explain their academic and personal motives in their own words and to connect the planned study or research project with their development. This supports a simple rule: motivation should be demonstrated through reasons and evidence, not adjectives.

Do not manufacture metrics

Technical metrics are useful only when they are real and explainable. If your robot achieved a measured localization error, state how it was measured. If you do not have a defensible number, describe the technical outcome without inventing “92% accuracy” or another impressive-looking statistic.

Common weak patterns

  • Repeating the CV paragraph by paragraph.
  • Calling the institution “world class” without showing specific fit.
  • Name-dropping professors whose work is unrelated to your proposal.
  • Using one document unchanged for every university.
  • Writing career promises that sound certain despite no supporting path.
  • Submitting text you cannot explain in an interview.

A practical editing test

  1. Highlight every sentence that claims a skill or achievement. Can you prove it?
  2. Highlight every sentence about the target programme. Is it current and specific?
  3. Remove generic praise that could fit 50 universities.
  4. Check every required prompt and word/page limit one final time.
  5. Read the document aloud and make sure the language sounds like something you would actually say.

Use templates as scaffolding, not evidence

A template can help you organise the document, but it cannot supply your reasons, research fit or achievements. ARVENAIRE’s free SOP resources are therefore starting frameworks; the final document should be specific to your real background and the target programme’s rules.
